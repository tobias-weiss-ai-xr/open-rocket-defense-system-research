How should Ukraine defend against Russian missiles?

Not with a single silver-bullet weapon system. The evidence from 10,000 probabilistic simulations and a 20,000-scenario Bayesian decision analysis points to a portfolio: passive protection first, an active layer traded like insurance, and expensive interceptors reserved for the targets that justify them.

01 The problem

Russia attacks Ukrainian cities with a mixed missile and drone arsenal, from hypersonic ballistic missiles to cheap one-way attack drones. Each threat class behaves differently, and no single air-defense system can stop all of them.

The core tension: a Mach 10 ballistic missile (Kinzhal) is effectively unhittable by any Ukraine-deployable interceptor, while a $48–193K Shahed drone can be shot down — but only if the interceptor costs less than the drone attacks it is buying off. Defenders must therefore decide what is worth shooting at.

Ballistic threats are many times faster than cruise missiles and drones — which is exactly why interception becomes physically impossible for them.

Warhead mass

Ballistic warheads carry 10× the explosive mass of a Shahed — the cost of a miss (or a fake alert) is not symmetric across classes.

02 Why simulate instead of guess?

Every number in this research is uncertain: missile unit costs have no reliable public source; interception probabilities are rarely observed; casualty rates per leak depend on timing and shelter behavior. Point estimates hide that uncertainty. Simulation makes it explicit.

📐 Monte Carlo (10,000 runs)

Draws capital costs, AI accuracy, network availability, EW effectiveness and daily threat rates from statistical distributions, then records cost, hit probability, lives saved and failure risk for every run. Output: the distribution of outcomes, not one fake-exact number.

0% of runs met the AI system’s 60% hit-rate target key result

🎲 Bayesian decision analysis (20,000 paired scenarios)

Compares five competing defense strategies on the same threat scenarios (paired draws), using Beta posteriors for interception probability, Gamma priors for costs and Gamma-Poisson for threat flows, over a discounted 10-year horizon. Output: honest posterior probabilities and cost-per-life HDIs for every strategy.

Paired design isolates strategy differences from shared uncertainty

🔎 Where doubt remains

A claim registry tracks all 36 quantitative claims with status. As of the last audit: 13 verified against primary sources, 2 estimates, and 21 unverified. Absolute numbers carry wide uncertainty; rankings are far more robust because comparisons are paired.

✅ Recommended portfolio (ordered build)

PHASE 1 · 0–6 MONTHS

Passive-first layer — $0.6–1B

Mass early-warning network + shelters + basic command & control. Protects against every threat class, including unhittable Kinzhal/Iskander. Deployable in ~6 months, cheapest per life saved in ~100% of scenarios.

PHASE 2 · 6–18 MONTHS

Conditional insurance — cruise layer + counter-drone + deception

Adds IRIS-T/NASAMS for cruise missiles, C-RAM/Gepard and small-drone interceptors for Shahed waves, plus deception. Buys a ~37% chance of saving ~39,000 additional lives exactly when the threat surges. Gate on observed escalation.

TARGETED · GATE-BASED

High-end terminal defense — critical nodes only

Patriot/SAMP-T batteries only around power, rail and command nodes — never city-wide. At ~$198K/life it is the worst city-wide option, but irreplaceable at a handful of nodes.

PARALLEL · R&D ONLY

AI-enabled distributed kinetic system — funded research

Prior Monte Carlo shows 0% chance of meeting the 60% hit-rate target; Bayesian analysis confirms it is dominated under every posterior. Keep as a research hedge with 18/30-month decision gates — never primary.

🔬 What new information is actually worth buying?

Perfect information about which strategy is best would leave the mean cost-per-life essentially unchanged (~0% improvement) — passive-first wins cost so decisively that oracle knowledge adds nothing. It could add only ~3.7% more lives saved. The highest-value data to collect is therefore not more system-cost trivia but threat-rate trends and real shelter-utilization rates, which decide when to trigger Phase 2.
